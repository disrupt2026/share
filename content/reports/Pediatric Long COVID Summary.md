# Pediatric Long COVID — Summary

*2026-11-19 · Thea*

Synthesis of key findings on pediatric long COVID from Zotero library.

## Prevalence and symptoms

Millions of children worldwide develop persistent symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Definitions vary — most studies require symptoms lasting ≥4–12 weeks — which complicates prevalence estimates across studies.

In a survey of 510 children (Buonsenso et al., 2022), fatigue, headache, and cognitive symptoms were the most common complaints, with the majority reporting reduced activity levels and mental health effects. A Barcelona cohort study (Gonzalez-Aumatell et al., 2022) found that among 46 children and young people with long COVID, the most prevalent symptoms were fatigue (87%), cognitive impairment (57%), and headache (52%), persisting at median 6 months follow-up.

Gastrointestinal symptoms are increasingly recognized: Wechsler et al. (2024) found that somatic, emotional, and GI symptom severity were significantly elevated in children and adolescents with COVID-19 compared to controls, suggesting post-infectious disorders of gut-brain interaction (PI-DGBI) as a distinct pediatric pathway.

A unified clinical definition remains elusive. The RECOVER EHR cohort (Botdorf et al., 2024) evaluated a computable phenotype against chart review in 651 children across 16 hospital systems; agreement was moderate (accuracy 0.62, PPV 0.49), partly because clinicians attributed symptoms to pre-existing conditions.

Meta-analyses of observational studies including >80,000 children report long COVID symptoms in 25% of children following SARS-CoV-2 infection (Joseph et al., 2023). Prominent symptoms include exercise intolerance, shortness of breath, and orthostatic intolerance. MIS-C represents the severe end of the pediatric spectrum: approximately 80% of MIS-C patients require ICU admission, ~50% exhibit left ventricular systolic dysfunction or myocarditis, 10–20% develop acute coronary artery aneurysms, and 4% require ECMO. A prospective multicenter study found persistent symptoms and activity intolerance at 2–4 months in 26.9% of children hospitalized with acute COVID-19 and 30% of those hospitalized with MIS-C. Notably, PASC symptoms in children appear independent of initial infection severity and can occur despite resolution of laboratory and echocardiographic abnormalities.

## Functional impact

Ford et al. (2025, CDC EID) found that among school-aged children in the US (2022–2023), those with long COVID had significantly higher rates of illness-related school absenteeism and functional limitations — including difficulty with physical activity and concentration — compared to children without long COVID.

The Gonzalez-Aumatell cohort (2022) documented reductions across social, academic, and health domains: 65% reported reduced school attendance and 43% required academic accommodations. Physical activity was curtailed in the majority, with many children unable to return to pre-illness activity levels at 6 months.

A qualitative study of children's and families' experiences (Faux-Nightingale et al., BJGP Open 2024) found that symptom unpredictability, school reintegration difficulties, and feeling disbelieved by clinicians were central concerns. Children described losing their sense of identity and struggling to communicate symptoms that fluctuated day to day.

Social determinants of health compound functional impact. Rhee et al. (JAMA Pediatrics 2026), drawing on the RECOVER-Pediatrics cohort (4,584 participants, 52 US sites), found that lower socioeconomic status, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic race/ethnicity, and reduced healthcare access were each independently associated with higher long COVID risk and severity.

## Care and clinical management

Fewer than 20 dedicated pediatric long COVID clinics operate in the US (Villatoro et al., 2025), creating substantial access barriers. An ECHO telementoring program trained community clinicians in long COVID management and found improvements in self-reported knowledge and comfort with diagnosis, though evidence on patient outcomes remains limited.

Kamel et al. (2025), reporting clinical insights from a single long COVID clinic, described a predominantly female adolescent patient population presenting with fatigue, headache, and orthostatic intolerance. Most required referrals to multiple subspecialties, underscoring the need for coordinated, multidisciplinary care.

Miller et al. (Open Forum Infectious Diseases 2026) described an interdisciplinary pediatric long COVID program and reported improvements in health-related quality of life across physical, emotional, and social domains after a structured care intervention. The program incorporated integrative approaches alongside standard medical management.

## RECOVER findings

The NIH RECOVER Initiative has produced the largest US pediatric long COVID evidence base to date, primarily through EHR data from 19 children's hospitals covering >1.8 million children.

**Cardiovascular outcomes.** Zhang, Thacker et al. (Nature Communications 2025) found elevated risks of hypertension, myocarditis, and ventricular arrhythmias in SARS-CoV-2-positive children and adolescents compared to negative controls (297,920 cases vs. 915,402 controls), with increased risk persisting 28–179 days post-infection and independent of pre-existing cardiac conditions.

**Kidney outcomes.** Li et al. (preprint, medRxiv 2024) reported that among children without prior chronic kidney disease, SARS-CoV-2 infection was associated with increased risk of CKD stage 2+ (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.13–1.28) and CKD stage 3+ (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.15–1.59) in the post-acute phase.

**Case identification.** Botdorf et al. (preprint, medRxiv 2024) found that an EHR-based computable phenotype identified long COVID with moderate accuracy (PPV 0.49), highlighting the difficulty of capturing pediatric long COVID through administrative data alone.

**MIS-C subphenotypes.** Jing et al. (preprint, medRxiv 2024) used a federated learning framework across nine US children's hospitals to identify three distinct MIS-C subphenotypes without sharing patient-level data.

## ME/CFS overlap and autonomic dysfunction

Siberry and Rowe (2022) reviewed the substantial symptomatic and mechanistic overlap between pediatric long COVID and ME/CFS, noting that post-exertional malaise, orthostatic intolerance, unrefreshing sleep, and cognitive impairment appear in both conditions. They argued that the existing ME/CFS literature — including evidence on activity management and orthostatic treatment — should inform pediatric long COVID care while awaiting condition-specific trials.

Weaver et al. (2026, NIH MCAM study) characterized pediatric ME/CFS across multiple sites and compared burden to adults. Children showed high rates of post-exertional malaise, fatigue, and cognitive symptoms, with functional impairment comparable to adult patients despite younger age at illness onset.

Orthostatic intolerance is a prominent feature in clinical long COVID cohorts. Kamel et al. (2025) found it among the most frequent referral reasons in their pediatric long COVID clinic, consistent with POTS and related dysautonomia as common presentations in adolescents. MIS-C survivors also carry significant neurologic and psychological burden at 2 years (Rollins et al., 2025), including cognitive and mood symptoms that overlap with ME/CFS and long COVID profiles.

Exercise intolerance in PASC and ME/CFS also share mechanistic underpinnings beyond symptoms. Joseph et al. (CHEST 2023) reviewed cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) findings and found that early framing of PASC exertional intolerance as deconditioning was not supported by the data. CPET reveals perturbations in systemic blood flow and ventilatory control — specifically low preload, impaired cardiac output, and ventilatory inefficiency — that are not typical of simple detraining and that substantially overlap with findings in ME/CFS.

Pediatric-specific exercise data from MIS-C survivors reinforces this. In 40 children followed at 6 months post-discharge, 45% had 6-minute walk test performance below the 3rd percentile for age and sex. In a smaller CPET subsample, all patients showed lower peak VO₂, impaired oxidative metabolism (reduced aerobic capacity at the anaerobic threshold and lower oxygen uptake efficiency slope), and ventilatory inefficiency (elevated VE/VCO₂). Critically, these impairments occurred in some patients with normal inflammatory markers and normal ventricular systolic function on echocardiogram, arguing against residual cardiac disease as the sole explanation.

Pulmonary function testing in 73 children following SARS-CoV-2 seroconversion showed no impairment except in those with severe infection — mirroring the normal PFT pattern seen in ME/CFS despite subjective breathlessness. Orthostatic intolerance is common in adolescents with PASC, with palpitations, dizziness, and lightheadedness frequently reported; some adolescents meet criteria for POTS. Joseph et al. recommend that evaluation include echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, PFTs, 6-minute walk test, CPET, and orthostatic testing.
